Three model releases landed within four days at the end of October, and they have little in common. OpenAI's GPT-6.1 Terra is a mid-priced model built to operate a computer. Google is handing Gemini 4 Carbon, its strongest model, to cyber defenders before anyone else, while Mistral has published the weights of Large 4, a trillion-parameter model trained in Europe.

For a marketing team, the useful question is which one to use for which job. Below is how we would split the work today, with the grey zones left visible.

## What actually shipped

**GPT-6.1 Terra (OpenAI, 26 October).** OpenAI's own page blocked our reader, so we rely on Simon Willison's write-up, which quotes the announcement. OpenAI describes Terra as "a fast, efficient model that combines detailed visual reasoning and precise computer use", with clearer writing. API pricing is 1 dollar per million input tokens and 5 dollars per million output tokens, roughly half the price of GPT-6.1 Sol, according to Willison. Ted Sanders of OpenAI, quoted in the same post, calls it a midrange option, similar to the flagship models on vision and computer use but much faster, and "a bit worse on most other things".

**Gemini 4 Carbon (Google, 28 October).** Google's blog post is explicit about access. Carbon goes first to "a subset of cyber defenders", with access then phased to trusted partners through the Fairwind Program. Developers, businesses and consumers will get it "as soon as possible", with no date. Introductory pricing is 2 dollars input and 10 dollars output per million tokens, rising to 4 and 20 afterwards. Google reports a first place on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, with a score of 59.

**Mistral Large 4 (Mistral, weights on 29 October).** The model had been in public preview earlier in the month. Mistral describes a one trillion parameter multimodal model, trained from scratch on European infrastructure, fluent in more than 160 languages. API pricing is 0.70 dollars input and 2.10 dollars output per million tokens. The weights are on Hugging Face under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight licence, a modified MIT licence. The release date of the weights comes from secondary coverage, including Vals.ai, because Mistral's page does not print one.

## The one you probably cannot use yet

Start with Carbon, because most comparisons skip the access question.

Unless your organisation is part of Google's Fairwind Program, you cannot plug Carbon into your content workflow this month. Google says plainly that it is still gathering feedback and iterating on guardrails before a wider release. Benchmarks of a model you cannot call are interesting reading and nothing more. For planning, assume Gemini 4 Argon stays your Google option until Google publishes a date.

There is a second point worth knowing before Carbon does open up. On Artificial Analysis's AA-Omniscience evaluation, Carbon at high reasoning scores the best accuracy on the board, 54, but its hallucination rate is 11%, against 4% for Argon at the same setting. Carbon at medium reasoning comes back down to 4%. Google, for its part, says its internal tests show Carbon cuts unsupported claims by about half compared with Argon.

Both statements can be true, because they measure different things. For marketing work, the practical reading is that a more capable model can still be more willing to answer when it should say it does not know. If you produce fact-heavy content, test the reasoning setting as carefully as the model. We covered how to set up that kind of check in our article on [hallucinations and client content](/en/blog/gpt-5-5-instant-hallucinations-trust-ai-content).

## Content production

For day-to-day writing, Terra and Mistral Large 4 are the realistic candidates.

Terra's pitch includes clearer, more natural writing, and OpenAI lists drafting proposals among its target tasks. At 1 and 5 dollars it is cheap enough to run several drafts per brief. The caveat comes from OpenAI itself. If Terra is a bit weaker than Sol on most things, long strategic pieces may still deserve the flagship.

Mistral Large 4 makes the stronger case for multilingual work. A model built and trained in Europe, claiming more than 160 languages, is worth testing on French, German or Spanish copy where English-first models sometimes produce text that reads as translated. We have not yet run it against our own briefs, so treat this as a hypothesis to test.

Our rule does not change with each release. Build twenty real briefs you have already delivered, run them through the candidate blind, and ask one question. Would you send this to a client after a light edit? Brand voice, client names and house conventions show up in that test long before they show up in a benchmark.

## Driving ad consoles and other screens

This is the job Terra was built for. Precise computer use means the model reads the screen and clicks, which opens tasks like pulling a report from a platform without an export, filling a repetitive form or checking settings across accounts.

We would use it, with limits. Wherever an API exists, an API call stays more reliable than a model clicking through an interface that changes every quarter. Computer use is the fallback for the tools that have no API, or a poor one.

The other limit is about money. A model that can click can also change a budget, a bid or a target audience. In our own setup, anything that spends money goes through a proposal that a person approves, and the value is read back after it is written. That rule matters more with a fast, cheap agent, because the cost of letting it loop is low and the cost of a wrong click is not.

## Sensitive data from a European client

When a client in a regulated sector asks where their data is processed, an answer such as "on our own servers in Europe, and it never leaves" settles most discussions. Mistral Large 4 is the only one of the three that allows it, because the weights can be downloaded and run with vLLM or SGLang on infrastructure you control.

Two cautions. First, the hardware. A trillion-parameter model needs data-centre GPUs, either owned or rented, and someone who knows how to keep inference running. We went through that trade-off in detail when Kimi K3 came out, in [our piece on open-weight frontier models](/en/blog/kimi-k3-open-weight-frontier-model-agency-decision), and the reasoning carries over.

Second, the licence. Mistral's open-weight licence allows commercial use and fine-tuning, but businesses that run a Model-as-a-Service offer need a separate commercial licence. An agency that runs the model for its own work is in a different position from one that sells hosted access to it. If you are anywhere near the second case, read the licence with a lawyer before building on it.

And if you use Mistral's hosted API instead of the weights, check the contract. The announcement stresses European training. It does not say where the hosted API processes data, and that is the point your client will ask about.

## Cost, per job

Per token, Mistral Large 4 is the cheapest of the three, and Carbon the most expensive. That ranking rarely survives contact with a real workflow, for two reasons.

First, output tokens dominate most marketing tasks. A content brief is short going in and long coming out, so the output price matters more than the input price. Terra's 5 dollars against Mistral's 2.10 is the comparison that counts for writing.

Second, a cheaper model that needs two retries costs more than a dearer one that gets it right the first time, and it also costs your reviewer's time. The number to track is cost per accepted output, and the only way to get it is to run your own test set and count.

| Model | Price per million tokens, in and out | Who can use it now | Where we would use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-6.1 Terra | $1 and $5 | API developers and ChatGPT subscribers, per OpenAI's Ted Sanders | Screen-based tasks, first drafts, proposals |
| Gemini 4 Carbon | $2 and $10, then $4 and $20 | Cyber defenders in the Fairwind Program | Not yet, wait for general access |
| Mistral Large 4 | $0.70 and $2.10 | API, OpenRouter, Microsoft Foundry, open weights | Multilingual content, sensitive European data |

## What we would do this month

Keep your current default model for most work. None of these releases forces a switch.

Add Terra to your test set for screen-based tasks and for first drafts, and give it a spending guard before it touches an ad account. Put Mistral Large 4 on the shortlist for any client whose contract says data stays in Europe, and price the hosting honestly before you promise it. Leave Carbon on the watch list until Google publishes a date, and when it does, test it at medium and high reasoning separately.
