On 27 October, Amperity announced that Stellantis had selected its customer data platform to support a customer data transformation across the group's brands and touchpoints. The press release, relayed by CBT News the next day, lists the targeted uses plainly. Cross-brand engagement, after-sales and loyalty, personalised messaging and campaign measurement.

For a dealer principal, the interesting word in that list is after-sales. The workshop is where a dealership knows its customers best, and it is exactly the kind of data a manufacturer platform wants to see.

The announcement says nothing about dealers' rights, consent or data returned to the network. We would not read anything sinister into that silence. The terms are probably not written yet, or at least not published, and that is the moment to start asking questions.

## What was actually announced

Here is what the release says, and only that. Amperity will let Stellantis recognise customers across brands, households and vehicles. Its architecture is described as open and composable, meant to work with the data tools Stellantis already runs rather than replace them.

The proof of concept gave the headline numbers. Amperity ingested millions of records in minutes and built a unified Customer 360 view in less than three days, tested on real Stellantis data and business requirements. As Unite.AI points out, these are evaluation results, not evidence of a completed rollout.

Kabir Shahani, Amperity's co-founder and co-CEO, summed up the problem in one sentence. "Automotive buyers span brands, vehicles, households, dealers, digital interactions and years of ownership."

Note the word dealers in that list. It is the only place the network appears. No regions, no named brands, no rollout timeline and no financial terms were disclosed. We will not guess at what Stellantis intends to do with the platform, and you should be wary of anyone who claims to know.

## What a dealership sends upstream

Most dealers underestimate how much customer data already leaves the building. Lead forms on brand websites, test drive bookings, warranty and recall work, connected vehicle services and satisfaction surveys often route through manufacturer systems by design.

The workshop is the richer seam. Every service visit records a vehicle, its mileage, the work done and the customer who brought it in. Over years, that history says more about a household than any campaign click. We wrote about why that history is so underused in our piece on [dealership after-sales and predictive retention](/en/blog/dealership-service-aftersales-predictive-ai-retention).

A unified platform on the manufacturer side makes this data more valuable, because it can be joined across brands. A customer who services a Peugeot at your site and leases a Jeep elsewhere becomes one profile. That is useful for the group. Whether it is useful for you depends on what comes back.

## What to get back, in writing

Before new data flows are switched on, a dealer group should be able to answer three questions, in writing.

**Leads, with their source.** When a lead generated by a group campaign lands in your area, does it reach you with the channel, the campaign and the timestamp? Without the source you cannot judge your own marketing spend.

**Service and vehicle signals about your customers.** If your workshop data feeds a central profile, ask what you receive in return. Due dates, recall status and contract renewals for customers attached to your site are the obvious candidates. If data only flows one way, the dealership carries the cost of collection and the group keeps the benefit.

**Consent records.** Under GDPR, consent is given for a purpose and to a controller. Consent collected by the manufacturer does not automatically let you email that customer about your own offers, and consent collected by you does not automatically extend to the group. Ask how consent is stored, who can see it, and how a withdrawal at one end reaches the other. Our article on [GDPR transparency enforcement](/en/blog/gdpr-2026-transparency-enforcement-marketing-forms) covers why regulators now check this first.

## Keep your own base alive

The manufacturer's data and yours can coexist. The risk is that a dealership stops investing in its own base because the group appears to do it better, and then discovers its customer relationships are on loan.

What keeps a base alive is ordinary work done consistently.

- Collect consent on your own forms, for your own purposes, worded clearly, and keep the proof with a date.
- Contact customers when you have something useful to say, typically a service reminder, an MOT or inspection date, or a trade-in estimate as the lease ends.
- Clean the file twice a year. Duplicates, dead emails and customers who sold the car years ago all cost money.
- Track which contacts come from your own channels and which come from the group, so you know which half of the relationship you own.

None of this is new. Our guide to [first-party data for dealerships](/en/blog/first-party-data-auto-dealerships-post-cookie) goes through the collection side in more detail. What has changed is the urgency, because the more the manufacturer centralises, the more your own file is the thing that makes your site different from the one twenty kilometres away.

## The objection we hear most

"The group's data will be better than ours anyway, so why bother?"

Sometimes it will be broader. It will rarely be more local. A manufacturer platform sees brands and vehicles across a country. Your file knows that a customer always books on a Saturday morning, prefers a call to an email, and asked about an electric replacement at the last service. That kind of detail is what turns a reminder into a booking.

There is also a practical point. Manufacturer programmes change with each new strategy cycle. Your own base, built on your own consent, survives those changes.

## What we would do this quarter

If we were sitting in a dealer group's marketing seat, we would do four things before the end of the year.

First, inventory the data that leaves the business, system by system, including the ones nobody thinks of, such as the workshop management software and the satisfaction survey tool.

Second, reread the dealer agreement and any data processing annex with one question in mind. Who is the controller for each flow, and what does the dealership receive in return?

Third, check your own consent records. Pick twenty customers at random and see whether you can show when and how each one agreed to hear from you. If you cannot, fix the forms before anything else.

Fourth, prepare the three asks above as a short written note for the next network meeting. Requests that arrive in writing, with examples, tend to get answers. Requests made in a corridor usually do not.

The Stellantis announcement is a technology choice, and a sensible one for a group with this many brands. For dealers, it is a reminder that customer data is part of the commercial relationship with the manufacturer, and it deserves the same attention as margins and targets.
