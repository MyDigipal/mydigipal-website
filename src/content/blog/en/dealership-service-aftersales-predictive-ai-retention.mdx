A dealership spends most of its digital budget chasing people who buy a car once every four to seven years. Meanwhile the workshop sees those same customers every twelve months, and almost nobody markets to them properly.

The numbers make this strange. Aftersales carries higher margin than vehicle sales in most groups, and the customer is already known, already in the database, already contactable. Yet the service department typically gets a reminder letter, a generic email blast, and whatever budget is left.

Industry data through 2026 shows what happens when that changes: personalised, predictive service reminders lift workshop visits by 10 to 20 percent.

## Why the workshop gets ignored

Three reasons, and none of them are about the value of the revenue.

Sales is visible. A vehicle sale is a discrete, celebrated event with a clear attribution story. A service booking is routine, so it never enters the marketing conversation.

The data lives somewhere else. Vehicle leads sit in a CRM the marketing team can access. Service history sits in the dealer management system, which marketing usually cannot query and often does not have a login for.

And the reminder already exists. Most dealerships send something, so the box feels ticked. What they send is a fixed-interval message that ignores how the customer actually uses the car.

## What predictive actually means here

Nothing exotic. It means using three fields most systems already store.

**Mileage trajectory.** A customer doing 30,000 kilometres a year needs servicing on a different rhythm than one doing 8,000. A calendar-based reminder is wrong for both.

**Vehicle age and known failure patterns.** Certain components come due at predictable points. A message that names the likely need converts better than one that says "time for your service".

**Past booking behaviour.** Someone who books three weeks after the reminder needs a different send date than someone who books the same day. And someone who has ignored four reminders needs a different message, or a phone call.

Combine those and you stop sending one message to everyone on the first of the month. You send the right message on the day it is most likely to be acted on.

## The three messages that actually get booked

Segmentation without better messages just sends the same email at a different time. These are the three that move the needle, in our experience.

**The named need.** Not "your service is due" but "at your mileage, most vehicles of this age need the brake fluid changed". It works because it tells the customer something they did not know, and because it makes the appointment feel specific to their car rather than to the calendar.

**The lapsed reactivation.** Aimed at customers who skipped their last service, and the hardest one to write. The instinct is to guilt them. The version that works acknowledges the gap without comment and gives a reason to come back now, usually a check that is free and takes twenty minutes. You are buying the visit, not the margin on that visit.

**The pre-purchase bridge.** Sent to customers whose vehicle is approaching the age or mileage where replacement becomes rational, and framed around the car they have rather than the one you want to sell. "Your vehicle is worth roughly this today" earns a reply more often than "discover our new range".

That third one is where aftersales and sales finally connect. The workshop knows the condition and history of the car. Sales usually does not. Passing that knowledge across is worth more than most acquisition campaigns.

## What the maths looks like

The reason this deserves budget is that the arithmetic is favourable in a way acquisition rarely is.

You already have the contact details. You already have the purchase and service history. There is no media cost to reach these people, only the cost of the message and the work to segment properly. The lift industry data points to, 10 to 20 percent more workshop visits, applies to a base you are not paying to reach.

Compare that with a vehicle acquisition campaign, where you pay for every impression against a buyer who may be five years from purchase.

The second effect is longer term and larger. A customer who keeps servicing with you is dramatically more likely to buy their next vehicle from you. Retention in the workshop is acquisition for the showroom, delayed by three to five years. Very few groups model it that way, which is precisely why the budget keeps going to the shorter, more expensive loop.

## The phygital point, without the buzzword

The word doing the rounds in 2026 is phygital: online booking and digital relationship joined to the physical workshop and the human contact. It is a clumsy word for a real idea.

The service relationship is the one place where a dealership still has a genuine advantage over a pure online player. The customer physically comes to you. They talk to someone. That contact generates trust that no advertising budget buys.

The failure is not the physical part. Workshops are generally good at the in-person moment. The failure is everything around it: the booking that has to happen by phone during working hours, the reminder that arrives at the wrong time, the follow-up that never comes, and the fact that nobody connects the service visit to the next vehicle purchase.

## Where to start, concretely

Four steps that do not require replacing the DMS.

**Export the service base and check its state.** Most groups discover that a meaningful share of records have no valid email, or a phone number that belongs to a previous owner. Cleaning that is unglamorous and it doubles the reach of everything you do afterwards.

**Segment on usage, not on date.** Three segments is enough to start: high mileage, standard, and lapsed. The lapsed segment, customers who skipped their last service, is usually the most profitable to reactivate and the one nobody contacts.

**Time the message per segment.** Not one send date for the whole base. This is where the lift comes from.

**Measure bookings, not opens.** An open rate tells you nothing about workshop revenue. If the booking is not tracked back to the message, you cannot defend the budget next year. That join is the same [tracking work](/en/services/tracking-reporting) that underpins everything else.

The email execution itself is not the hard part, and our [email marketing team](/en/services/emailing) handles that side routinely. The hard part is getting service data out of the DMS in a usable shape, and agreeing who owns the relationship once the customer is in the workshop.
