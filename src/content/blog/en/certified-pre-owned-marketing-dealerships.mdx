Most car shoppers say they would consider a certified used car. Far fewer could tell you what makes one certified. That gap, measured by Cox Automotive in a study published on 2 November, is a marketing problem that dealers can fix with their own website, feeds and sales team.

The figures come from the US market, and we will come back to what that means for a European or French dealer.

## What Cox Automotive measured

The 2026 Cox Automotive Certified Pre-Owned Vehicle Study surveyed 825 US consumers planning to buy a vehicle within six months. Its headline numbers are blunt. 57% of all shoppers say they are open to buying a certified pre-owned vehicle. Certified transactions account for roughly 12% of retail used-vehicle sales.

The explanation Cox puts forward is understanding. Among new-vehicle shoppers open to certified pre-owned, only 31% say they know exactly how it differs from a regular used car. Among used-vehicle shoppers open to it, the figure drops to 27%. Roughly two-thirds of respondents were unclear on what the acronym itself stands for.

Interest and transactions are different measures, so the distance between 57% and 12% is not a count of lost sales. Some shoppers who are "open" will never buy, and dealers can only certify the cars that qualify for a manufacturer programme. Still, the direction is clear, and Cox's own reading is that "the market does not lack interest in CPO vehicles", in the words of Vanessa Ton, senior manager of research and market intelligence.

The appeal of certified cars has held steady at 74%, while the appeal of new vehicles has slipped to 76% from 85% in 2023, with the average new-vehicle price above $50,000. And 72% of current certified owners say they would never go back to a traditional used car once they have experienced the benefits.

People who understand the product tend to stay with it, so the work is getting them to understand it before they choose.

## Before you apply it to Europe

These are US shoppers talking about US programmes. We have not found a comparable study for France or the wider European market, so we would not quote these percentages to a French dealer group as if they were local.

What carries over is the structure. Many manufacturers run an approved used car programme in Europe too. In France, Stellantis sells under Spoticar, whose listings show Premium labels with 12 or 24 months of warranty and Advanced labels with 8 months. Volkswagen's programme, Volkswagen Occasions Garanties, promises a 90-point inspection and at least 12 months of warranty. BMW runs Premium Selection. Each comes with its own checklist, warranty terms and return conditions.

Those details are exactly what a shopper cannot guess from a badge. Whether French buyers understand them better than Americans is an open question that your own data can answer. How many of your used-car leads ask about the warranty? How often does a salesperson have to explain the label from scratch?

If the answer is "often", the US pattern probably applies to you.

## Start with the vehicle page

Every channel you run sends people to one place, the vehicle detail page. That is where the label has to be explained, because nobody reads a programme's terms on a separate page.

Here is what we would put next to the price on every certified car:

- the programme name, written out in full rather than as a logo alone;
- what was checked, with the number of inspection points if the programme publishes one;
- how long the warranty runs and who backs it, the manufacturer or the dealer;
- the return or exchange terms, if the programme offers them.

Keep it to four short lines, which a shopper comparing cars on a phone will actually read.

Then add a filter. If your stock search lets people sort by fuel and gearbox but not by certified status, you are hiding the cars you most want to sell at their full price. A dedicated landing page listing only certified stock also gives your campaigns somewhere specific to land.

Finally, check that the badge in the photos matches the text. The same consistency applies to your local pages and your Google Business Profile, which our guide to [local SEO for dealerships](/en/blog/local-seo-tactics-that-win-dealership-traffic) covers in detail.

## Put the label in your Vehicle Ads feed

Google Vehicle Ads has a dedicated attribute for this, `certified_pre-owned`. It is optional, it takes "yes" or "no", and Google's help page says it "should be used when the car is OEM certified pre-owned". Google also recommends populating it with the relevant value for each vehicle.

Two consequences follow. Fill it for every used car in the feed, with "no" where it applies, so the data is complete. And reserve "yes" for manufacturer programmes. A dealer's in-house warranty can be a strong argument, and it belongs in the ad copy and on the page rather than in this field.

Google has also tightened its pricing rules for Vehicle Ads. If you have not reviewed your feed since, our article on [Vehicle Ads pricing rules](/en/blog/google-vehicle-ads-pricing-transparency-dealers) is the place to start, because a certified flag on a feed that fails price checks will not get you far.

## Campaigns that lead with the explanation

Most used-car ads lead with model, mileage and price. For certified stock, the label has to work harder than that, and the copy should say what it means in one line. "Manufacturer-checked, 12-month warranty" tells a shopper more than "Certified" ever will.

On search, we would build a separate campaign or ad group for certified stock, with its own keywords around the programme name and the idea of a warranted used car. Someone who types a programme name deserves an ad and a landing page that match.

On Meta, a catalogue product set filtered on certified cars lets you run dynamic ads that only show those vehicles, with a creative that carries the explanation. Retarget the people who viewed a certified car page, since they have already shown interest in the label.

Cox's data also points to an audience worth adding. Its figures show shoppers drifting away from new cars on price, while the appeal of certified cars holds. Someone pricing a new model and hesitating is a candidate for a certified alternative of the same model.

## The sales floor has to say the same thing

A website that explains the label well loses its effect if the salesperson describes it differently. Give the team the same four lines that appear on the vehicle page, and ask them to use them word for word in the first conversation.

The objection they will hear most is about price. Why pay more for a certified car than for a similar one without the label? The answer has to be concrete, in terms of what was checked, how long the cover lasts and who pays if something fails. If your team cannot say it in thirty seconds, the shopper will not repeat it to their partner that evening either.

Certified buyers can also come back. A warranty serviced through the manufacturer network gives them a reason to return to your workshop, which makes them potential after-sales customers. That link is worth keeping in mind when you calculate what a certified sale is worth, and our article on [after-sales digital strategy](/en/blog/automotive-aftermarket-digital-strategy-service-revenue) explains how to follow it through.

| Where | What to show | What to measure |
|---|---|---|
| Vehicle page | Programme, checks, warranty, return terms | Leads per certified page view |
| Vehicle Ads feed | `certified_pre-owned` filled for every used car | Clicks and leads on certified listings |
| Search and Meta | One line explaining the label | Cost per lead on certified stock |
| Showroom | The same four lines, word for word | Share of certified cars in used sales |

## How to know whether it worked

Measure before you change anything. Take the last three months and note the share of certified cars in your used-car leads and sales, the average time a certified car stays in stock, and the margin compared with similar non-certified vehicles.

Then roll the changes out in order, vehicle page first, feed second, campaigns third. Give each step a few weeks, since used-car volumes are small and one good week proves nothing.

If the share of certified cars in your used sales rises and stock rotation improves, the explanation is doing its job. If nothing moves after two months, look at the price gap first. Some shoppers will understand the label perfectly and still decide it is not worth the difference.

## Our view

The manufacturer provides the certified programme, but the dealer decides how clearly it is explained. Cox's figures suggest that, in the US at least, most of that explanation is missing.

We would start with the vehicle page this week. It costs an afternoon of copywriting, and every campaign you run afterwards will land on a page that does part of the selling.
